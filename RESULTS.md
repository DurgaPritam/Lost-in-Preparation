## What the results support

**Planned headline: the modern-methods audit (Phase 5).** For each study-set species, it will check how modern studies prepared the plant (solvent, temperature and filtration, each recorded separately) against how the historical recipes made it, and flag remedies that were never tested the way they were made. It has now been run on the 238 species–indication pairs of the Phase 5 species with a rule-based extractor and a protocol fixed before retrieval (headline: 99% (95% CI 94%–100%); nKnown = 67, nUnknown = 277). Its coding accuracy awaits the 40-study human hand-check.

**The preparation model is a transparent hypothesis generator, not a validated predictor.** Every factor and assumption is inspectable, and every estimate carries a range. Its support differs by preparation:
- **Essential-oil compounds: best supported.** In the pre-registered v1 check, 18/18 essential-oil rows agree with the model. These rows test presence only; no essential-oil row reported a compound as absent.
- **Aqueous preparations (infusions, decoctions, juices): unvalidated.** The pre-registered v2 check found 2 `absent/low` rows against a quota of 15 (agreement 0/2). The paired v3 check is **inconclusive** (5 articles). Single-row checks also measure detection, not the extraction fraction the model predicts (METHODS §9).
- **Wide ranges.** With descriptor error included, the median width of the 5th–95th range across study-set compounds is 0.45 for cold water infusion, 0.44 for hot water infusion (tea), 0.35 for decoction in water on a 0–1 scale.
- **Ranking.** Hypotheses will be ranked only on profile gaps that hold across those ranges (METHODS §11). Compounds whose range spans most of the scale cannot drive a rank.

**A finding from the screening itself.** Of 610 article screenings (549 distinct articles) across 40 plants, only **6** directly compared two preparations of the same plant material in a usable table: 6 of the 257 articles whose tables were examined after the licence and title checks. Within this sample, this is evidence that the literature rarely reports preparation effects in a form that can be compared (details under the v3 results).

Against the criteria fixed before the model was run, artemisinin is a **borderline pass**. The criteria were: the heat flag fires, the cold-water median is below 0.30, and the low-temperature nonpolar median is above 0.60. All three hold for the medians. The result is *borderline*, not a clean pass, because the 5th–95th ranges cross the thresholds.

Drivers are the median per-factor multipliers. The cold-water infusion is filtered (filtration 1.00), so its value comes from the solvent factor alone; the unfiltered variant shows how much the suspension assumption would add.

**Sweet wormwood tea: a disagreement explained by extraction vs detection.** The pre-registered v2 check found a report of artemisinin **present** in *Artemisia annua* (hot water infusion (tea); PMC13471599, CC BY 4.0):

> However, the total artemisinin yield of these infusions is exquisitely sensitive to edaphic conditions, harvest timing, post-harvest drying protocols, and storage duration: a metabolomic survey of teas cultivated across ten locations in Benin and one Mediterranean site documented artemisinin concentrations ranging from approximately 0.3 mg/L to 15.7 mg/L, with post-harvest losses of 24–48% measured over a single …

The model's median for this preparation is **0.16** (0.02–0.52), below the 0.40 `present` threshold, so the row scores as a **disagreement**.

The model and the paper answer different questions:
- The model estimates the **fraction** of the plant's artemisinin that a hot-water infusion carries: a minority fraction, because artemisinin is fairly lipophilic and its endoperoxide is heat-sensitive.
- The paper reports that artemisinin is **detected** in the tea at measurable concentrations. That depends on the fraction, but also on how much artemisinin the leaves contain and on the assay's detection limit.

A minority fraction of a high-content plant can still give a clearly measurable tea. LOTUS has no abundance data, so the model cannot predict detection, and this row is not evidence that the predicted fraction is wrong (METHODS §9, 'Extraction is not detection'). It is also not evidence that the fraction is right. A test of the fraction needs a paired comparison within one plant and study, which is what v3 does.

v3 found no table with paired artemisinin measurements, so the paired test does not reach artemisinin.

##### Screening finding: the literature rarely compares preparations of the same material

The v3 rule screened 610 search results (549 distinct articles) across 40 plants. Only **6** directly compared two preparations of the same plant material in a usable table. That is 6 of the 257 articles whose tables were examined (2%); the others failed the licence or title check first:

| Screening outcome | Articles |
|---|---|
| no eligible table | 251 |
| title does not name the plant | 223 |
| no CC licence | 118 |
| excluded: used in an earlier check | 12 |
| eligible (usable paired table) | 6 |

Within this sample, this is evidence that modern studies rarely test or report preparation effects in a comparable form. That is the gap the modern-methods audit (Phase 5) is designed to measure. The rule set a cap of 3 articles per plant, which was never reached, and the rule was not relaxed.

**Limits of the screen.**
- It used only the first page of two PMC searches per plant.
- It required a Creative Commons licence and the plant's name in the title.
- It read only HTML tables: comparisons reported in text, figures, images or supplementary files count as "no eligible table".

So the screen shows that usable comparisons are scarce. It does not show that none exist.

### Why cold water gives artemisinin 0.27 (diagnostic; no model change)

Source: `data/interim/m4/water_calibration.json`; figure: [docs/figures/water_likelihood_vs_logp.svg](docs/figures/water_likelihood_vs_logp.svg).

- A filtered, cold, aqueous preparation has heat = filtration = acidity = 1, so the prediction is the water window alone: `f = sigmoid(k * (upper - logP)); no solubility, melting-point, dose or contact-time term`, with upper ~ U(1.0, 2.5) and k ~ U(1.0, 2.0). For the study-set compounds it is a one-variable function of logP: Spearman ρ = -0.9973 (n = 2257; 26 compounds sit below the curve because of the MW > 1000 size penalty).
- The median crosses 0.40 at logP 2.004, 0.30 at logP 2.297 and 0.10 at logP 3.278. The 'undecided' band 0.30–0.40 is only 0.29 logP units wide, narrower than the descriptor's RMSE against experiment (0.8374), and the median study-set compound has logP 2.432, on the steepest part of the curve.
- Artemisinin's Crippen logP is 2.395, and sigmoid(k_med · (upper_med − logP)) = 0.2754 reproduces the Monte Carlo median of 0.2749. At the experimental logP 2.9 (PubChem CID 68827, LogP (SangsterLogP)) the same model gives 0.158 (0.0129–0.6172). Part of the 0.27 is descriptor error; the rest is the window's position and shallow slope.
- **Low water solubility is not in the model.** It sees only logP, with no solubility, melting-point (crystal-lattice), dose or contact-time term, so it cannot represent 'poorly soluble in absolute terms'. A low amount in a large volume of water may still dissolve completely. PubChem's artemisinin record lists no experimental solubility, so no solubility value is used here.
- **Proposal (not adopted).** Fix the window slope at ln 10 (the slope of a two-phase partition equilibrium) and model descriptor error explicitly. This was pre-registered as the secondary variant and evaluated only on v2 rows (above). A solubility/dose term would need per-compound melting points or solubilities and plant-content ranges that the repo does not have; it is not proposed yet.
- The recorded artemisinin result and every threshold are unchanged. Separately, the v2 rows include a published report of artemisinin present in *Artemisia annua* (hot water infusion (tea), PMC13471599), which the primary model scores 0.16 (disagrees); see the artemisinin section above.
